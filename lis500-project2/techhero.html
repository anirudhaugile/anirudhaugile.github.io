Joy Buolamwini

Founder, Algorithmic Justice League

Joy Buolamwini is a computer scientist and digital activist. While she was a graduate student at the MIT Media Lab, she found that face-tracking software couldn't detect her dark skinned face until she put on a white mask. That moment pushed her to study how AI systems treat different people.

Her research project, Gender Shades, tested commercial facial analysis tools from major tech companies. The tools were nearly perfect for lighter skinned men but made far more mistakes on darker skinned women. Several of the companies improved their systems after the results came out.

In 2016 she founded the Algorithmic Justice League, an organization that helps to raise awareness about AI harms. She has testified before Congress, appeared in the documentary Coded Bias, and wrote the book Unmasking AI (2023).

Her work is a clear example of intersectionality in technology. Looking at gender alone or race alone would have hidden the problem. The biggest errors showed up for people who were both women and darker skinned. It also shows how implicit bias gets built into systems. Nobody had to intend harm. The training data just didn't include enough people who look like her.

Timnit Gebru

Founder and Executive Director, Distributed AI Research Institute

Timnit Gebru is a computer scientist and AI researcher whose work focuses on bias, accountability, and the people affected by automated systems. She earned her PhD in computer vision at Stanford and later worked on ethical AI research at Microsoft and Google.

Gebru also co-founded Black in AI, which works to increase the presence and visibility of Black people in artificial intelligence. She later founded the Distributed AI Research Institute, or DAIR, to support independent research that is not controlled by the priorities of large technology companies.

I chose Gebru because her work connects technical research with questions about who has power in AI. Her work pushes back against the idea that bigger or more automated systems are automatically better. It also connects to intersectionality because she asks whose experiences are represented in data, who is most exposed to harm, and who gets a voice when technology is designed.
